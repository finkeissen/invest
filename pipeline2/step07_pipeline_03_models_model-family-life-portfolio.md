# Model Family: Life Portfolio

## Purpose

This model family represents how a person or group allocates scarce resources across multiple life domains under uncertainty.

It is the central model family for turning broad investment philosophy into practical planning.

---

## Core objective

To model how actors choose, sequence, and review investments across domains such as:
- health,
- education,
- work,
- savings,
- relationships,
- meaning,
- institutional participation,
- optionality.

---

## Main variables

- current condition by domain
- declared goals
- constraints
- available resources
- headwinds
- tailwinds
- dependencies between actions
- expected gains
- expected downside
- maintenance load
- review cadence

---

## Key mechanisms

### 1. Multi-domain allocation

Actors distribute finite resources across domains rather than solving a one-domain optimization problem.

### 2. Dependency-sensitive sequencing

Some investments only perform well when earlier prerequisites are already stable.

### 3. Maintenance pressure

Existing capacities decay unless maintenance is funded.

### 4. Overconcentration risk

Excessive investment in one domain may weaken the whole portfolio.

---


## Protective extension

The life portfolio is not only an upside-seeking allocation model.

It is also a protection and recoverability model.

Actors must allocate scarce resources not only toward future gain, but toward preserving the minimum conditions of a good life, reducing fragility, and maintaining the ability to recover after shocks.

This includes protection against: 
- health shocks,
- accidents and disability,
- income interruption,
- relationship breakdown,
- institutional and social instability,
- financial crises,
- other events that can push the portfolio below critical thresholds.

---

---

## Additional mechanisms

### 5. Threshold protection

The portfolio must protect minimum viable conditions before chasing marginal upside.

### 6. Shock buffering

Some allocations function primarily as buffers rather than return-generating assets.

Examples can include emergency savings, social support, insurance-like arrangements, spare capacity, or health reserves.

### 7. Recoverability design

A robust portfolio does not assume shocks can always be prevented.

It also asks whether the actor can regain function, income, trust, or stability after losses.

### 8. Second-order review

Protective strategies themselves must be reviewed for blind spots, overconfidence, displaced burdens, and maintenance cost.

---

## Main failure modes

- sacrificing prerequisites for upside,
- leaving critical thresholds unprotected,
- hidden maintenance starvation,
- overloading attention and energy,
- pretending all gains are commensurable,
- confusing buffering with wasted capital,
- mistaking formal coverage for real recoverability,
- ignoring effects on close others.
