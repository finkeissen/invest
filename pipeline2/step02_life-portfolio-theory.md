# Life Portfolio Theory

## Purpose

This document translates the broad investment philosophy of Invest-GT into a practical portfolio framework for real persons and groups.

A life portfolio is the ordered set of investments by which an actor tries to build, preserve, and improve future condition across multiple domains.

---

## Why a portfolio view is needed

People do not invest only in one thing.

They simultaneously allocate scarce resources across:
- health,
- education,
- work and income,
- financial reserves,
- relationships,
- meaning and orientation,
- institutional and civic commitments.

A portfolio view prevents over-optimization of one domain at the expense of the rest.

---

## Portfolio construction sequence

A practical life-portfolio process should proceed in this order:

1. define goals,
2. define constraints,
3. define current condition,
4. identify available domains,
5. generate candidate investments,
6. score gain, downside, and prerequisites,
7. sort by sequence and dependency,
8. choose a manageable portfolio,
9. define execution rhythm,
10. review and revise.

---

## Core portfolio domains

A minimal domain taxonomy for life portfolios:
- health
- mental stability
- education and skill
- work and income
- financial resilience
- relationships and trust
- meaning and spiritual orientation
- character and habit structure
- civic and institutional participation
- strategic optionality

Not every actor will weight these equally.

---

## Constraints

Any executable portfolio must explicitly represent constraints such as:
- money,
- time,
- energy,
- health,
- dependents,
- geography,
- social obligations,
- institutional constraints,
- legal constraints,
- moral constraints.

---

## Portfolio heuristics

Useful default heuristics include:
- protect basic stability before pursuing upside,
- remove severe headwinds early,
- prefer compounding investments when prerequisites are met,
- do not buy upside by destroying core capacities,
- preserve optionality where uncertainty is high,
- include maintenance, not only growth,
- keep review points explicit.

---

## Execution and review

A portfolio is not complete when written down. It becomes real only when paired with:
- a sequence,
- a cadence,
- stop rules,
- escalation rules,
- revision triggers,
- evidence about what is or is not working.

This is the practical bridge between theory and life execution.

---

## Life portfolio as protection architecture

A life portfolio should not be understood only as a set of growth-oriented investments.

It is also a protection architecture for preserving the conditions of a good life under uncertainty.

This means that a portfolio must be able to do at least four things:
- enable future improvement,
- preserve core capacities,
- absorb shocks,
- support recovery after loss.

A person does not only invest for gain.
A person also invests so that illness, accidents, financial shocks, dependency events, or wider social disruption do not destroy the basis of action entirely.

---

## Risk classes that portfolios must observe

A practical life portfolio should distinguish at least between:
- acute personal risks,
- chronic degradation risks,
- household and financial risks,
- relationship and care risks,
- institutional and societal risks.

Examples include serious illness, accidents, burnout, loss of income, debt spirals, divorce, care burdens, inflation, institutional breakdown, or violence.

Not all risks can be prevented. But many can be reduced, buffered, transferred, or made more survivable.

---

## Modes of protection

A strong life portfolio uses more than one protection mode.

The main modes are:
- prevention,
- mitigation,
- buffering,
- transfer,
- recovery,
- adaptation.

Prevention tries to reduce the probability of loss.
Mitigation tries to reduce severity.
Buffering creates reserves and slack.
Transfer shifts part of the burden to insurance, institutions, or other actors.
Recovery improves the ability to regain function after damage.
Adaptation changes the portfolio structure when conditions have already changed.

---

## Minimum security before expansion

In many real situations, the right first move is not maximization but the securing of minimum conditions.

That may include:
- bodily safety,
- sleep and health maintenance,
- minimum financial runway,
- legal and administrative order,
- relationship reliability,
- access to care,
- retained earning capacity,
- preserved decision quality.

The portfolio should therefore ask not only "what yields more upside?" but also "what prevents unacceptable collapse?"

---

## Life-phase shifts

Portfolio composition should change over the life course.

Examples:
- early phases may emphasize skill acquisition, health foundations, and option creation,
- family phases may emphasize stability, redundancy, insurance, and care capacity,
- later phases may emphasize preservation, simplification, intergenerational transfer, and protection against dependency.

This means a life portfolio is dynamic rather than fixed.
The same allocation logic should not be applied unchanged across decades.

---

## Second-order observation

A protection-oriented portfolio must also observe itself.

Otherwise an actor may:
- overinvest in unlikely threats,
- neglect invisible chronic decline,
- mistake purchased products for real resilience,
- create maintenance burdens that weaken life instead of securing it,
- become so defensive that growth, meaning, and relationship quality erode.

For that reason, each portfolio review should include a second-order question:

> What is this portfolio protecting against, what is it failing to see, and what costs are being hidden by the current protection logic?

This keeps risk management from turning into unobserved ideology.
