# Model Family: Risk and Reversibility

## Purpose

This model family represents downside, uncertainty, lock-in, and recovery potential.

A practical investment framework must not only estimate upside. It must distinguish reversible mistakes from irreversible damage.

In the extended direction of the framework, this model family also represents threshold protection, catastrophic exposure, and the difference between nominal coverage and real recoverability. It therefore supports not only risk scoring but the design of portfolios that remain livable under personal, financial, and systemic shocks.

---

## Core objective

To classify actions by:
- downside size,
- downside concentration,
- reversibility,
- recovery time,
- uncertainty level,
- optionality preserved or destroyed.

In the extended formulation, the model should also classify actions by:
- threshold exposure,
- irreversibility risk,
- buffering capacity,
- recoverability after shock,
- protection coverage,
- fragility transfer,
- false-safety risk.

---

## Main variables

- probability of failure
- severity of failure
- reversibility class
- recovery cost
- time to detect failure
- optionality retained
- dependence on optimistic assumptions
- critical threshold crossed if failure occurs
- degree of irreversibility if threshold is crossed
- buffering capacity available before damage compounds
- recovery resources required after shock
- protection mechanisms already in place
- protection gaps remaining
- fragility transferred to others or to the future
- false-safety indicators

---

## Key distinctions

### Reversible vs. irreversible

A wrong but reversible action is often preferable to a slightly better-looking but irreversible one.

### Fragile upside vs. robust adequacy

Some actions offer large upside but collapse under small shocks. Others offer modest gain but strong resilience.

### Visible risk vs. hidden risk

A stable-looking action may hide health, trust, or maintenance risk that arrives later.

### Covered vs. recoverable

Formal protection is not identical to real recovery. A person can appear covered on paper while still lacking time, trust, liquidity, health, or institutional access needed to recover.

### Local shock vs. threshold event

Some failures remain local and repairable. Others push the system across a threshold after which the relevant baseline cannot be restored cheaply, quickly, or at all.

### First-order vs. second-order protection failure

A protection measure can reduce one risk while generating another one through dependence, maintenance burden, illusion of safety, or harm displacement.

---

## Protective interpretation

Under the broader life-portfolio framing, this model family should be used to ask:
- which thresholds must not be crossed,
- which losses are irreversible or near-irreversible,
- which shocks can be buffered,
- which failures can realistically be recovered from,
- which protections merely look adequate,
- where protection is absent, fragile, or transferred.

This makes the model family relevant not only for selecting opportunities but for deciding what must be defended before expansion is pursued.

- risk class
- reversibility class

- optionality score or classification
- stop-rule recommendation
- threshold-risk map
- irreversibility warning
- buffering assessment
- recoverability profile
- protection coverage classification
- false-safety warning

---

## Main failure modes

- chasing upside under hidden fragility,
- misclassifying irreversible commitments as ordinary bets,
- ignoring recovery capacity,
- confusing low volatility with low risk.
- treating nominal coverage as equivalent to lived protection,
- failing to identify threshold-crossing scenarios,
- overestimating recovery where trust, health, time, or coordination are missing,
- reducing one risk by displacing fragility elsewhere,
- becoming over-defensive in ways that preserve safety signals while degrading life capacity.

---

## Minimal modeling questions

Any use of this model family should be able to answer at least:
- What is the relevant downside?
- What threshold could be crossed?
- How reversible is that threshold crossing?
- What buffers exist before damage compounds?
- What would recovery actually require?
- What is formally covered, and what remains exposed?
- What new fragilities does the protection itself create?
- What signs would indicate false safety?
