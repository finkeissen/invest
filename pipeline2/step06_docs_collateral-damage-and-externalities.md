# Collateral Damage and Externalities

## Purpose

This document gives Invest-GT an explicit place for dealing with harms, side effects, distributional conflicts, and tragic trade-offs.

A framework that speaks about gain without making harm legible will drift into moral fog.

In the extended life-investment direction of the repository, this also includes the harms created by attempts at protection, security, and resilience-building.
A strategy that claims to protect a person, household, or group may still externalize damage, shift fragility elsewhere, or impose hidden burdens on others.

---

## Core claim

No serious investment framework is complete unless it records:
- who benefits,
- who pays,
- who carries risk,
- who is excluded,
- who can object,
- what damage is tolerated.

This applies not only to expansionary investments, but also to defensive and protective investments.
Attempts to reduce one risk can create new dependencies, new exclusions, or new forms of fragility.

---

## Main distinctions

### Positive-sum, zero-sum, and destructive outcomes

An outcome may:
- create net new value,
- redistribute value,
- destroy value while appearing locally beneficial.

These cases should not be conflated.

### Direct and indirect harms

Harms may be:
- direct,
- delayed,
- distributed,
- hidden,
- reputational,
- institutional,
- intergenerational.

### Concentrated and diffuse effects

A group-level improvement can still be morally or strategically defective if the gains are diffuse while the harms are concentrated on identifiable persons.

### Protective gains and displaced harms

A person or group may improve its own safety while increasing risk for others.
Examples include:
- offloading financial risk,
- shifting care burdens,
- hardening one node while weakening another,
- preserving local stability through wider exclusion,
- buying private resilience while relying on degraded public systems.

A protective investment is not automatically justified by the fact that it reduces danger for the focal actor.
Its wider damage pattern still has to be made visible.

### First-order and second-order protection failures

Some strategies fail because they do not protect enough.
Others fail because they protect narrowly while creating hidden second-order damage.

Examples include:
- preserving income by destroying health,
- preserving household stability by exhausting one caregiver,
- preserving local order by raising systemic fragility elsewhere,
- building redundancy that becomes unaffordable to maintain,
- reducing one tail risk while increasing dependence on brittle institutions.

A framework for resilience must therefore also track the collateral damage of resilience strategies.

---

## Lesser-evil logic

Some choices involve real tragedy rather than clean optimization.

When all available options involve some damage, records should state:
- what harms attach to each option,
- why the chosen option was judged less damaging,
- what mitigation is planned,
- what repair obligations remain.

This is especially important when defensive action protects one threshold only by crossing another.
Not every protection choice is cleanly preservative; some are merely less ruinous than the alternatives.

---

## Minimum review questions

Every major investment or strategy should answer:
- Who gains?
- Who loses?
- Is the loss avoidable?
- Is the loss reversible?
- Is there a lower-harm path?
- Are the affected persons visible in the record?
- Does the gain rely on moral externalization?

For protective or resilience-oriented strategies, records should also ask:
- What risk is being reduced?
- What new burdens are created by the protection itself?
- Who must maintain the protective structure?
- What dependencies does the protection create?
- Does the strategy shift fragility across persons, institutions, or time?
- Does it create false safety?
- What recovery obligations remain if the protection fails?

---

## Recording standard

A minimally adequate record for collateral damage in Invest-GT should make visible:
- the focal actor being protected or advanced,
- the persons or groups bearing cost,
- the time horizon of harm,
- whether the harm is reversible,
- whether the harm is consented to,
- whether the harm is repairable,
- whether the strategy merely displaces risk rather than reducing it,
- and what review trigger should cause the decision to be revisited.

This helps keep both gain and protection from disappearing into rhetorical abstraction.
