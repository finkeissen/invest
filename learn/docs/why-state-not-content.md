# Why State, Not Content

A short explanation of the framework's central design decision.
This document is non-normative. It explains; it does not oblige.

---

## The default model: content delivery

Most learning systems are organised around content.

A learner is presented with material, asked to engage with it,
assessed on their engagement, and moved to the next unit.
The system's primary unit is the lesson, the chapter, the card,
the topic. The learner appears in this model mainly as the recipient
of content, and progress is measured by how much content has been
covered.

This model has obvious operational advantages.
Content is producible, schedulable, and scalable.
A teacher or system can be evaluated by what was covered, what was
attempted, and what was completed.

---

## What the default model misses

It misses where the learner actually is.

Two learners may have completed the same material with the same
nominal results and yet stand in entirely different positions:

- one understands the underlying structure and can apply it to new
  problems; the other can recall standard examples but cannot
  reconfigure them;
- one has a clear idea of what they don't know; the other is
  unaware of significant gaps;
- one is being held back by a missing prerequisite from years ago;
  the other is learning fluently and would benefit from more
  challenge.

A content-organised system has no native vocabulary for these
differences. It records what was delivered. It does not record
where the learner stands.

---

## The alternative: state

This framework treats the learner state as the primary object.

The state is structured: it records not only *which* concepts have
been engaged, but at *what depth* (the dimension shift scale), and
*against what target* (the goal context).

Content becomes secondary. Content is what a learner interacts with
in order to change their state. Whether a particular content item
is good or appropriate is judged by its observable effect on the
state, not by its position in a syllabus.

This inversion has consequences:

- A learner who has covered nothing on a topic but can demonstrate
  level 2 on it is not a gap.
- A learner who has covered everything on a topic but can only
  demonstrate level 0 is a clear gap, regardless of the coverage.
- An intervention is judged by whether it shifts the state, not by
  whether it was delivered.
- A teacher's effort is recorded; a learner's state is the outcome.

---

## Why this matters for transparency

State is auditable in a way that coverage is not.

A coverage record answers: *what did the system do?*
A state record answers: *where is the learner now?*

For a learner trying to understand their own position, the second
question is the one that matters.
For a system trying to act responsibly, the second question is the
one it must keep honest.

This is why the framework defines learner state, gap, dimension
shift, and intervention as contracts before defining anything else.
Without those contracts, "where is the learner" has no stable
meaning, and the system can only report what it has done — which
is exactly what most systems already do.

---

## What this design does not solve

State-centred design does not, on its own, produce good interventions,
strong probes, or useful curricula.

It produces the **conditions under which** those things can be
honestly evaluated.

The interventions, probes, and curricula themselves are operational.
They are specified elsewhere.
They are not the contribution of this repository.

The contribution of this repository is structural:
a vocabulary in which the question *where is the learner* has an
admissible answer, and where the absence of an answer is recorded
rather than fabricated.
